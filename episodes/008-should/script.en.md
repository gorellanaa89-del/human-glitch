So let's put a face to that word.

Meet The Should, the voice that turns every wish into a duty and every rest into a debt.

It doesn't shout. It sounds reasonable, almost wise, like a strict teacher who only wants the best for you. "You should have done more. Other people manage it, so why can't you?"

And because it sounds so reasonable, we rarely question it. We just assume it's right, and that we're the problem. And the more we obey it, the louder and more demanding it gets.

The Should especially loves responsible people, the ones who grew up being the good kid, the one who never caused problems.

So how can you tell it's with you? Four signs show up a lot. Your to-do list never ends, because every item you cross off brings two more. Resting feels like cheating. You say I should far more often than I want. And you're harder on yourself than you'd ever be on a friend.

If you recognized two or more, don't worry. It doesn't mean something is wrong with you. It just means The Should has been working overtime.

> set: open_office

And here's the twist: all those shoulds don't make Sarah do more. They make her do less.

On Monday morning, the report she should have finished is still open on her screen. It's been there for two weeks, and every time she looks at it, it feels less like work and more like a punishment, so she checks one more email instead.

That's a pattern psychologists see again and again: when we do something because we feel we have to, we tire out and give up sooner than when we do it because we want to. By turning the report into a duty, The Should drains the very energy Sarah needs to finish it.

And The Should doesn't stay inside your head.

That afternoon, Daniel brings Sarah a draft one day late, and before she can stop herself, she says it: "You should have sent this yesterday."

Daniel's face drops, and Sarah regrets it instantly. It's the same tone she uses on herself every morning, and hearing it out loud, aimed at someone else, she realizes how harsh it really is.

That's the thing about the shoulds we use on ourselves: little by little, the rules we live by become the rules we judge everyone else by.

# Scene: Why it exists
> part: why
> keywords: savanna tribe landscape group
> card: THE RULES OF THE TRIBE
> cast: bug:should:neutral
> set: savanna
> note@1: GUILT | an alarm to repair

But The Should is not your enemy. For our ancestors, it worked more like a guardian.

In a small group on the savanna, nobody survived alone, and every group had its rules: share your food, do your part, don't let the others down. And breaking those rules was dangerous, because whoever lost the group's trust could lose its protection too, and alone on the savanna, that could cost you your life.

So guilt evolved as an alarm. It goes off when you break a rule and pushes you to repair things fast, to apologize, pay back and try harder. The ones who felt that alarm kept their place in the group, and with it, their chances of survival. And children who learned the group's rules quickly were safer, so our brains became experts at absorbing rules long before we're old enough to question them.

Today, though, the rules come from everywhere: your parents, your first boss, and hundreds of strangers on your phone who seem to run ten kilometers before breakfast and never miss a deadline.

The guardian can't tell a real rule from an imported one, so it treats all of them as if your place in the group depended on it. That's why a single unanswered email can feel like a small moral failure. The alarm isn't broken; it's just trying to protect you from a thousand rules that nobody really agreed on.

And that's the update. It isn't about dropping your standards, but about choosing which ones are really yours. Here are four ways to do it.

First, catch it. Notice every time you think the word should, because you can't question a rule you don't even hear.

Second, ask whose rule it is. If it's yours and you still believe in it, keep it. If it belongs to someone else, you're allowed to give it back.

Third, swap should for could, or for want. I should go to the gym becomes I want to go, because I sleep better when I do. It sounds small, but it turns a duty into a choice, and choices are much easier to keep.

And fourth, talk to yourself like you'd talk to a friend. It won't make you lazy: in a 2012 study at Berkeley, students who failed a hard test and were encouraged to treat themselves kindly then studied longer for the next one.

So try it right now: think of one should that's been following you this week. Whose voice is it, and how would it sound as a could?

A week later, on the evening train, Sarah's list has seven lines, and most of them start with want. Then she calls her mom, not because she should, but because she wants to.

And for the first time in months, the call feels like a gift instead of a chore.

The Should is still there, sitting on her shoulder, but smaller and quieter now, more like a reminder than a judge.

So, a quick recap. The Should turns your wishes into duties and your rest into guilt. It exists because, for our ancestors, following the group's rules meant staying in the group. And you update it by catching the word, asking whose rule it is, swapping should for could, and being a friend to yourself.

Because you are not a list of rules you keep failing. And you don't have to earn your rest.
