So let's put a face to that word.

Meet The Should, the voice that turns every wish into a duty and every rest into a debt.

It doesn't shout. It sounds reasonable, almost wise, like a strict teacher who only wants the best for you. "You should have done more. You should be better by now. Other people manage it, so why can't you?"

And this voice is not new at all. Back in 1950, the psychoanalyst Karen Horney gave it a name that stuck: the tyranny of the shoulds. She noticed that many of her patients weren't living their lives; they were trying to live up to an ideal version of themselves, and punishing the real one every time it fell short.

Decades later, in 1987, a psychologist named Tory Higgins tested this idea with a theory he called self-discrepancy. He asked people to describe three versions of themselves: who they actually are, who they would love to be, and who they believe they ought to be.

Then he looked at the gaps. And he found something interesting. When people fell short of the person they would love to be, they mostly felt sad or disappointed.

But when they fell short of the person they believed they ought to be, the feeling was different. It was anxiety, guilt and restlessness, a kind of constant alarm that something bad was about to happen.

That second gap is exactly where The Should lives. It doesn't make you dream bigger; it makes you feel like you're in trouble, all the time, with no one in particular.

Now, The Should doesn't visit everyone equally. It especially loves responsible people, the ones who grew up being the good kid, the reliable one, the one who never caused problems.

So how can you tell it's with you? There are four signs that show up a lot.

Your to-do list never ends, because every time you cross something off, two more appear. Resting feels like cheating, so even on vacation you check your email. You say I should far more often than I want. And you're harder on yourself than you would ever be on a friend.

If you recognized two or more, don't worry. It doesn't mean something is wrong with you. It just means The Should has been working overtime.

# Scene: Why it backfires

And here's the twist. You would think all those shoulds would make Sarah do more, but they actually make her do less.

On Monday morning, the report she should have finished is still open on her screen. She has had it for two weeks, and every time she looks at it, it feels less like work and more like a punishment, so she checks one more email instead.

Psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan spent decades studying exactly this. When we do something because we feel we have to, to avoid guilt or to look good, we tire out faster and give up sooner. But when we do it because we value it or want it, the same effort lasts much longer.

So The Should has a strange effect: by turning the report into a duty, it drains the very energy Sarah needs to finish it.

And The Should doesn't stay inside your head. Sooner or later, it starts talking to other people too.

That afternoon, Daniel brings Sarah a draft one day late, and before she can stop herself, she says it: "You should have sent this yesterday."

Daniel's face drops, and Sarah regrets it instantly. It's the same tone she uses on herself every morning, and hearing it out loud, aimed at someone else, she realizes how harsh it really is.

That's the thing about the shoulds we use on ourselves. We rarely keep them to ourselves; little by little, the rules we live by become the rules we judge everyone else by.

# Scene: Why it exists

But The Should is not your enemy. For our ancestors, it worked more like a guardian.

Picture a small group living on the savanna, where nobody could survive alone. Every group had its rules: share your food, do your part, don't let the others down. And breaking those rules was dangerous, because whoever lost the group's trust could lose its protection too.

That's why guilt evolved. It's an alarm that goes off when you break a rule, and it pushes you to repair things quickly: to apologize, to pay back, to try harder. The ones who felt that alarm kept their place in the group, and with it, their chances of survival.

And there's more. Children who learned the group's rules fast were safer, so our brains became experts at absorbing rules from parents and elders, often long before we're old enough to question them.

Today, though, the rules don't come from one small tribe. They come from your parents, your first boss, your school, and from hundreds of strangers on your phone who seem to wake up at five, run ten kilometers and never miss a deadline.

The problem is that the guardian can't tell a real rule from an imported one. So it treats all of them as if your place in the group depended on it, and sounds the alarm every time you fall short.

That's why a single unanswered email can feel like a small moral failure. The alarm isn't broken; it's just trying to protect you from a thousand rules that nobody really agreed on.

And that's the update. It isn't about dropping all your standards, but about choosing which ones are really yours. Here are four ways to do it.

First, catch it. Just notice every time you think the word should, because you can't question a rule you don't even hear.

Second, ask whose rule it is. If it's yours and you still believe in it, keep it. If it belongs to someone else, you're allowed to give it back.

Third, swap should for could, or for want. I should go to the gym becomes I could go to the gym, or I want to, because I sleep better when I do. It sounds small, but it turns a duty into a choice, and choices are much easier to keep.

And fourth, talk to yourself like you would talk to a friend. You might worry that being kind to yourself makes you lazy, but the research says the opposite.

In a 2012 study, Juliana Breines and Serena Chen gave students a very hard test, so almost everyone did badly. The students encouraged to treat the failure with kindness then spent more time studying for the next test than the ones who simply got an ego boost.

So try it right now. Think of one should that has been following you this week. Whose voice is it, and how would it sound as a could?

The Should is still there, sitting on her shoulder, but now it's smaller and quieter, more like a reminder than a judge.

So, a quick recap. The Should turns your wishes into duties and your rest into guilt. It exists because, for our ancestors, following the group's rules meant staying in the group. And you update it by catching the word, asking whose rule it is, swapping should for could, and talking to yourself like a friend.

Because you are not a list of rules you keep failing. And you don't have to earn your rest.
