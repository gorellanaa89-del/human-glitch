The people with the lowest scores did better than only about twelve percent of the group. But they guessed they had beaten sixty-two percent.

Here's the trap. The skills you need to do something well are the same skills you need to notice you're doing it badly. When you don't know much, you also don't know what you're missing.

Researchers still debate how big this effect really is, and part of it may be a statistical quirk. But the core idea keeps showing up: confidence and skill are not the same thing.

# Scene: Why it exists
> part: why
> keywords: savanna landscape sunrise
> card: AN OLD ADVANTAGE
> cast: bug:know_it_all:neutral
> set: savanna
> note@2: CONFIDENCE = STATUS | Anderson, Berkeley 2012

But The Know-It-All is not a villain. Once, it was a real advantage.

Imagine two ancestors deciding where to hunt. One hesitates. The other points and says "there, I'm sure." The group follows the one who's sure. Confidence got people followers, partners, and a bigger share of the food.

Research at Berkeley found something similar today: overconfident people are often seen as more competent, and gain status, even when they're not better at the task.

Sometimes, acting before you're certain was the only way to act at all. Waiting for full information could mean losing the hunt.

# Scene: The update
> part: update
> keywords: notebook desk planning
> card: FOUR UPDATES
> cast: richard:neutral, bug:know_it_all:neutral
> set: office
> note@0: 1. NAME IT | "That's The Know-It-All"
> note@1: 2. EXPLAIN IT | step by step
> note@2: 3. ASK | what would prove me wrong?
> note@3: 4. KEEP SCORE | predictions vs results

That's the update. You don't silence your confidence. You give it a check-up. Four ways.

One: name it. When you feel completely sure, say: "That's The Know-It-All talking." Certainty is a feeling, not a fact.

Two: explain it step by step. Studies show that when people try to explain exactly how something works, like a zipper or a toilet, they discover how little they really know. If you can't explain the steps, you don't know them yet.

Three: ask "what would prove me wrong?" and then ask the person most likely to know. Usually it's the quietest person in the room.

Four: keep score. Write down your predictions and check them later. Richard said two weeks. It took fourteen. That number is better than any opinion.

Try it now. Think of something you're completely sure about at work. Ask yourself: what would prove me wrong, and who would know?

Quick recap. The Know-It-All makes you feel sure, especially where you know the least. It exists because confidence once won followers and food. You update it by naming it, explaining step by step, asking what would prove you wrong, and keeping score.

Being sure is not the same as being right. The best leaders know the difference.
