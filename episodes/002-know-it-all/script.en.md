So let's give that certainty a face.

Meet The Know-It-All.

It's the voice that whispers: "You already know this. Asking is for beginners. And doubt? Doubt is weakness."

Back in 1999, two psychologists at Cornell, Justin Kruger and David Dunning, decided to put this to the test. They gave people quizzes on logic, grammar and even humor, and then asked them to guess how well they'd done.

And here's what they found. The people with the lowest scores did better than only about twelve percent of the group. But when you asked them, they guessed they'd beaten sixty-two percent.

Why does that happen? Well, the skills you need to do something well are the same skills you need to notice you're doing it badly. In other words, when you don't know much, you also don't know what you're missing.

Now, to be fair, researchers still debate how big this effect really is, and part of it may be a statistical quirk. Even so, the core idea keeps showing up: confidence and skill are not the same thing.

But here's the thing. The real cost isn't the delay. It's the team.

Think about it. Daniel stopped warning him after that first laugh. Margaret stopped sending numbers. Little by little, people learned that bad news wasn't welcome, and so it simply stopped arriving.

And that's what The Know-It-All really does to a leader. It doesn't just make him wrong. It quietly removes the people who could have told him.

Researchers who study teams, like Amy Edmondson at Harvard, call this psychological safety: the feeling that you can speak up without being punished for it. And when it disappears, mistakes don't stop. They just go underground.

# Scene: Why it exists
> part: why
> keywords: savanna landscape sunrise
> card: AN OLD ADVANTAGE

> set: savanna
> note@2: CONFIDENCE = STATUS | Anderson, Berkeley 2012

Still, The Know-It-All is not a villain. In fact, a long time ago, it was a real advantage.

Picture two of our ancestors deciding where to hunt. One of them hesitates. The other one points and says: "There. I'm sure." So who does the group follow? That's right, the one who's sure. And that's how confidence brought people followers, partners, and a bigger share of the food.

Interestingly, research at Berkeley found something similar today. Overconfident people are often seen as more competent, and they gain status, even when they're not actually better at the task.

And back then, acting before you were certain was sometimes the only way to act at all. If you waited for all the information, you could lose the hunt.

The problem is, the world changed. A hunt was simple, and you saw the result the same day. A software project, on the other hand, has a thousand invisible parts, and the mistakes only show up months later.

So in a world like that, sounding sure stops being a strength. Instead, it becomes a risk, because nobody checks your work until it's too late.

In other words, the voice isn't broken. It's just using an old map in a new city.

And notice something else. The more senior you get, the fewer people tell you that you're wrong. So the voice gets louder exactly when you need it to be quieter.

# Scene: The update
> part: update
> keywords: notebook desk planning
> card: FOUR UPDATES
> cast: richard:neutral, bug:know_it_all:neutral
> set: office
> note@0: 1. NAME IT | "That's The Know-It-All"

> note@2: 3. ASK | what would prove me wrong?
> note@3: 4. KEEP SCORE | predictions vs results

And that's the update. You don't have to silence your confidence. You just give it a check-up. Here are four ways to do it.

First, name it. Whenever you feel completely sure, tell yourself it's The Know-It-All talking. Because certainty is a feeling, not a fact.

Second, explain it step by step. Studies show that when people try to explain exactly how something works, like a zipper or a toilet, they suddenly discover how little they really know. So if you can't explain the steps, you probably don't know them yet.

Third, ask yourself what would prove you wrong. And then go ask the person most likely to know. More often than not, it's the quietest person in the room.

And fourth, keep score. Write your predictions down and check them later. Richard said two weeks, remember? It took fourteen. And that number is worth more than any opinion.

So try it right now. Think of something at work you're completely sure about, and ask yourself: what would prove me wrong, and who would know?

Because in the end, being sure is not the same as being right. And the best leaders know the difference.
