Let's give it a face.

Meet The Blamer, the voice that, the moment something goes wrong, scans the room for someone to point at, long before it ever looks in the mirror.

And here's what makes it so frustrating to deal with. Richard doesn't feel like he's lying. When The Blamer speaks, he honestly believes it was Daniel's fault, because the voice rewrites the story in his head before he even has time to check the facts.

In other words, when you argue with a person like this, you're not arguing with someone who knows the truth and hides it. You're arguing with a memory that has already been edited.

Back in 2004, a team of psychologists led by Amy Mezulis reviewed two hundred and sixty-six studies, with children, adults and people from many different cultures. And they found the same pattern almost everywhere: when things go well, we take the credit, and when they go badly, we look for something outside of us to blame. Psychologists call it the self-serving bias.

So yes, The Blamer visits everyone a little. But in some people it practically moves in, especially in people under a lot of pressure, people with a lot of status to lose, and people who grew up in homes where making a mistake was never allowed.

So how do you spot The Blamer in someone you know?

Four signs show up again and again. They apologize with a but, as in sorry, but if you had told me earlier. Their stories always have a villain, and it's never them. They remember your mistakes in perfect detail, and somehow forget their own. And when you finally bring proof, they don't change their mind. They change the subject.

If two or more of those just reminded you of a specific person, you're not imagining it. You've simply been living next to a very loud voice.

# Scene: Why it exists
> part: why
> keywords: savanna tribe landscape group
> card: THE DEFENSE LAWYER
> cast: bug:blamer:neutral
> set: savanna
> note@1: REPUTATION = SURVIVAL | in a small tribe

Still, The Blamer is not a villain. In fact, for our ancestors, it worked more like a bodyguard.

In a small tribe, your reputation was your survival. If the group saw you as the one who lost the food, broke the spear or scared off the hunt, you could lose your place, and alone on the savanna, people didn't last long. So the brain developed a kind of defense lawyer, one that jumps in the second something goes wrong and argues your case before anyone else can accuse you.

On top of that, feeling capable kept our ancestors trying. Someone who blamed himself for every failed hunt might stop hunting, so a little self-protection kept people going.

# Scene: The update

> card: FOUR UPDATES
> cast: daniel:neutral, richard:neutral, bug:blamer:neutral
> set: office
> note@1: 1. LOWER THE THREAT | never corner them in public
> note@2: 2. WHAT, NOT WHO | talk about the fix
> note@3: 3. FACTS, CALMLY | in writing, in private
> note@4: 4. LEAVE AN EXIT | let them save face

And that's the update. You can't silence someone else's Blamer, but you can stop feeding it. Here are four ways.

First, lower the threat. The voice gets louder when it feels cornered, so never try to prove a Blamer wrong in front of an audience.

Second, talk about the what, not the who. Ask what happened and how to prevent it, not whose fault it was, because once nobody is on trial, the lawyer has nothing to defend.

Third, bring the facts calmly, in private and in writing. Something like: here's Friday's email, how do we make sure this doesn't happen again?

And fourth, leave them an exit. People admit far more when admitting doesn't cost them their dignity.

But one more thing. If a face popped into your head while you were watching, that's fine. Just check, once in a while, whether it's the face in your mirror. So next time something goes wrong, notice what your mind does first: does it look for a fix, or for a culprit?

So, a quick recap. The Blamer makes people point outward the second something goes wrong. It exists because, for our ancestors, a damaged reputation could mean losing the group. And you deal with it by lowering the threat, talking about the what instead of the who, bringing facts calmly and leaving an exit.

Because the people who never admit a mistake aren't the strongest people in the room. And the bravest sentence at work is often the shortest: that one was mine.
